What Lifeline can—and cannot—do.

A plain-language account of the prototype’s logic, evidence, privacy safeguards, and unfinished work.

Honest status

What works today

  • Real accelerometer and orientation reading on a phone.
  • Two-signal rule before any alarm, to cut false alarms.
  • Twenty second countdown with a cancel button.
  • Browser position, with accuracy shown in metres.
  • A message written for a contact you saved, opened in your own app.
  • No account, no login, no server. Everything stays in the browser.
  • No breathing or pulse sensing. This does not detect an overdose.
  • No automatic call to 911 and no responder network.
  • Dashboard figures are illustrative placeholders, not measurements.

Detection

How the rule works

A sudden movement above 2.5 g starts a check. That alone does nothing, because a dropped phone gives the same reading. A second signal is needed: a tilt of more than 45 degrees, or eight seconds with almost no movement. Any real movement resets the stillness clock, so a person who gets up again is never reported. Each signal carries points, and the total is shown on screen as a confidence figure, so nothing about the decision is hidden.

Thresholds live at the top of app.js as named constants: IMPACT_MS2, STILL_MS2, STILL_MS, COUNTDOWN_S and MIN_SIGNALS. They were chosen by hand and tested on [device names]. They are not validated against clinical data.

Privacy

What we hold and where

The contact name and number stay in the browser storage of the device, under the key pulseguard.contact.v1. Nothing is uploaded, because there is no server. The position is read only when you press the button or when an alert is prepared, and it is never stored. On the risk map, zones are 500 m across and any zone with fewer than five calls is removed, so the map cannot point at one household.
